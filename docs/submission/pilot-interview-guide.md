# Kavu pilot discovery and interview guide

**Status:** proposed research. No interviews, participants, findings or partner commitments are represented by this document.

**Aim:** learn whether drying coordination is painful enough for a cooperative or aggregator to adopt and pay for Kavu. Discover the operator's actual work before explaining the proposed solution.

**Participants to recruit later:** yard supervisors, clerks or attendants, managers who approve spending, and a grain-quality or extension professional. Include at least one operator who believes their current notebook process works well.

## Thirty-minute discovery interview

### Opening: 2 minutes

Suggested words: “I’m researching how maize yards organize drying and record moisture. I’d like to understand a recent lot from arrival to storage. I’m not asking you to buy anything. Please avoid sharing farmer names or personal information. May I take notes?”

Request separate consent before any recording, photographing documents or quoting a participant. Never imply university, NCPB or WFP endorsement.

### Understand actual work: 12 minutes

- Walk me through the last maize lot you handled. When did it arrive, and what happened next?
- Who decided where to put it? How was available space assessed?
- How did attendants know what work to do? What happened when the person in charge changed?
- How and when was moisture measured? How were samples selected, and where was the reading recorded?
- Tell me about the last time rain, dew or changing conditions interrupted drying. What did the team actually do?
- Was there a time when a lot's history was difficult to reconstruct? What made it difficult?
- Which parts of the current process work well and should stay as they are?

Avoid leading questions such as “Would automatic weather alerts save you money?” Ask for specific recent examples before general opinions.

### Understand money and adoption: 8 minutes

- What alternatives are available when open-air drying is unsuitable?
- How are those services priced? Can an anonymized recent receipt or quote be examined with permission?
- What labour, transport, covering and handling costs must be included in a comparison?
- Who can approve a software purchase? What approval process and budget cycle apply?
- Which phone, computer and connection would the operator actually use? Are shared devices common?
- What has caused previous software or recordkeeping changes to fail?

Do not convert a hypothetical willingness-to-pay answer into revenue evidence. Ask about actual purchasing behavior and the next decision-maker.

### Show one workflow: 5 minutes

Only now show a short Kavu lot journey. Ask the participant to narrate what they think the screen means. Ask them to find a task, record a sample reading and explain the tariff-equivalent label.

Observe confusion without coaching. Record where help was required. Ask: “Which part would you remove?” and “What would prevent this from being used tomorrow?”

### Close: 3 minutes

Summarize their process and ask whether it is accurate. If there is interest, describe a small shadow-mode pilot and its boundaries. Ask who else needs to be involved. Do not claim that interest is a signed commitment.

## Research record template

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Interview ID and date | Anonymous identifier, not a farmer name |
| Participant role and approximate operation size | With permission; use a range if appropriate |
| Consent | Notes / recording / quotation / document inspection separately |
| Current workflow | Observed or described; identify which |
| Recent concrete problem | What happened, evidence available, alternative explanations |
| Current tools and costs | Source: receipt, estimate, memory or public tariff |
| Adoption constraints | Device, language, training, connectivity, decision authority |
| Strongest objection | Participant's meaning without embellishment |
| Hypothesis changed | What the team will change because of this interview |
| Follow-up permission | Explicitly requested, no implied authorization to send messages |

Keep direct quotes short, consented and exact. An anonymized paraphrase is preferable where identification could be sensitive. Never populate this template with fictional results for the pitch.

## Pilot design after discovery

Start with up to three willing yards and a small set of lot histories. The number is an initial feasibility target, not a powered study of loss reduction.

1. **Baseline observation:** record existing work and costs without changing handling.
2. **Shadow recommendations:** compare Kavu's suggestions with the supervisor's decisions; log disagreements and stale-data cases.
3. **Supervised use:** let operators use task allocation after local review, retaining existing protective practices and override ability.
4. **Review:** reconcile actions, measurements, receipts and user feedback; decide whether another cycle is justified.

## Measures to collect

| Measure | Why it matters | Important limit |
|---|---|---|
| Eligible lots entered and tasks completed | Whether it fits everyday work | Clicks do not prove physical work occurred |
| Time from instruction to recorded completion | Handover and coordination | Recording delays may differ from work delays |
| Moisture sampling and recheck frequency | Evidence quality | Meter condition and representative sampling matter |
| Rewetting episodes | Potential operational failure | Weather, sampling differences and prior handling can confound |
| Administrative time per lot | A possible buyer benefit | Measure a comparable process and include training time |
| Actual drying/handling receipts | Real financial comparison | A tariff equivalent alone is not a receipt or savings |
| Overrides and reasons | Whether recommendations fit local practice | Disagreement can reveal a useful local exception |
| Continued use and renewal choice | Commercial signal | Stated interest is weaker than paid continued use |

The outcome may be that a notebook remains sufficient or that hardware, training or labour is the larger constraint. Treat that as a valid finding. A startup should solve a problem customers actually have.
